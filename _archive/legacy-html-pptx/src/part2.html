Part 2 · Science into action

What can science actually do for maritime policy?

Measure the problem. Compare possible responses. Test whether an idea can work in practice.

16
[11:00–11:03 · 3 min] Part 1 explained why I became a researcher. Part 2 asks a harder question: what is research useful for? In maritime decarbonisation, science has at least three roles. It can measure the problem. It can compare possible responses. And it can test whether an attractive idea has a realistic chance of working. Science does not make the final policy decision. But without evidence, it is difficult even to agree on the problem, let alone choose a credible response. Start with observation

We can see the ships. We cannot see their emissions.

Looking outside tells us that ships are moving. It does not tell us how much they emit—or where the largest impacts occur.

17
[11:03–11:06 · 3 min] If you look outside the port, you can see many ships: container ships, ferries, tugs and workboats. But emissions are largely invisible. Simply counting ships is not enough. A large vessel travelling slowly may have a different impact from a smaller vessel working hard. A ship at berth is different from one crossing the ocean. Rhetorical prompt: if emissions are not directly visible, what information would we need before we could estimate them? [Sources] - IMO, Fourth IMO Greenhouse Gas Study 2020: https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/environment/pages/fourth-imo-greenhouse-gas-study-2020.aspx - Image: AI-generated illustrative pixel art, 2026-07-30.
Turn activity into an estimate

So how do we estimate what a ship emits?

Ask three intuitive questions: How hard is it working? For how long? What fuel is it using?

18
[11:06–11:10 · 4 min] The engineering calculation can become technical, but the basic logic is simple. First: how hard is the ship working? A ship moving fast or manoeuvring may require more power. Second: for how long? A short harbour movement and a multi-day voyage are not equivalent. Third: what fuel and engine are being used? Different combinations produce different amounts of greenhouse gases. In research, ship activity data, vessel characteristics and emission factors help us answer these questions. The formula is not the main message. The main message is that observable activity can be translated into a defensible estimate. Evidence changes the scale

The estimate changes the conversation.

1,056 million tonnes of CO₂ from shipping in 2018—about 2.89% of global human-caused CO₂.

19
[11:10–11:13 · 3 min] Once thousands of vessel-level estimates are combined, the issue changes from “there are many ships” to a measurable global climate problem. The Fourth IMO Greenhouse Gas Study estimated that international and domestic shipping produced 1,056 million tonnes of carbon dioxide in 2018, around 2.89 percent of global human-caused CO₂ emissions. That number does not tell us which policy to adopt. But it provides the scientific basis for saying that shipping must be part of the global decarbonisation effort. From why to how

Science helps us compare possible pathways.

Efficiency, operational change, alternative fuels and port infrastructure can all contribute. No single option fits every route.

20
[11:13–11:17 · 4 min] If the evidence says shipping needs to decarbonise, the next question is how. Possible pathways include improving vessel efficiency, changing speed or routing, using lower- or zero-emission energy, and building compatible port infrastructure. Science can compare technical performance, lifecycle emissions, cost, safety and operational fit. It can also show why there is no universal answer: a solution suitable for a short ferry route may not suit a trans-Pacific container service. Rhetorical prompt: which option sounds easiest—and what might be missing from that first impression? The implementation gap

A good idea can still fail in the real world.

Cost, fuel supply, vessels, infrastructure, safety, regulation and demand must line up.

21
[11:17–11:20 · 3 min] A pathway may look excellent in a model and still fail in implementation. A shipowner may hesitate to order a new vessel before fuel is available. A fuel producer may hesitate to invest before demand is certain. A port may need new infrastructure and safety procedures. Cargo owners may not accept the additional cost. This is a coordination problem. Technology is necessary, but technology alone is not enough. Narrow the problem

A Green Shipping Corridor creates a place to start.

Focus willing ports, ship operators and fuel partners on decarbonising one shared route.

22
[11:20–11:24 · 4 min] A Green Shipping Corridor narrows a system-wide problem to a specific route. The Clydebank Declaration describes corridors as zero-emission maritime routes between two or more ports. In practice, a corridor creates a space where willing actors can coordinate vessels, fuel, infrastructure and cargo demand. It is not automatically green because it has been announced. It is a structured attempt to move from ambition toward deployment. [Sources] - UK Government, COP 26 Clydebank Declaration for green shipping corridors: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cop-26-clydebank-declaration-for-green-shipping-corridors/cop-26-clydebank-declaration-for-green-shipping-corridors - Global Maritime Forum, Annual Progress Report on Green Shipping Corridors 2025: https://globalmaritimeforum.org/report/annual-progress-report-on-green-shipping-corridors-2025/ - Image: AI-generated illustrative pixel art, 2026-07-30.
The corridor is an ecosystem

The route is simple. The coordination is not.

A viable corridor must connect cargo, vessels, fuel, ports, finance, standards and timing.

23
[11:24–11:27 · 3 min] On a map, a corridor is one line between two ports. In reality, it is an ecosystem. Cargo creates demand. Vessels need compatible engines. Fuel must be produced, transported and bunkered. Ports need infrastructure and safety rules. Finance has to support investment. Standards must be accepted across jurisdictions. And all of these decisions need to happen on a compatible schedule. This is why many corridor initiatives reach a feasibility wall. Coordination becomes the central challenge. Return to evidence

This is where maritime data becomes useful again.

Data can reveal routes with real traffic, suitable vessels and commercial relationships strong enough to support a corridor.

24
[11:27–11:31 · 4 min] This is where my own kind of research comes back into the story. Instead of drawing an attractive line on a map, maritime data lets us ask whether a route has a real operational and commercial foundation. How much traffic already exists? Which vessel services are involved? Is there stable cargo demand? Which ports and companies already have a working relationship? Where might emissions reductions be concentrated? Research cannot guarantee that a corridor will succeed. But it can identify stronger candidates and reduce early uncertainty. A route with a commercial story

The Hong Kong–San Antonio route already moves something real.

The Cherry Express connects Chilean cherry exports with Hong Kong and Mainland China markets. Existing cargo makes a corridor proposal more credible.

25
[11:31–11:35 · 4 min] The Hong Kong–San Antonio connection has a concrete commercial story: cherries. The Cherry Express is a seasonal direct service bringing refrigerated containers of Chilean cherries to Asian markets. Port San Antonio reported that the 2023–24 season moved 11,776 containers and that direct services reduced transit time from about 28 to 22 days. Modern Terminals reported handling about 6,500 cherry containers in Hong Kong in January 2025. The key point is not the fruit itself. It is that this is not an imaginary route. Existing cargo, vessels, schedules and business relationships provide a more credible starting point for exploring a Green Shipping Corridor. From analysis to dialogue

Research helped move the idea into a policy conversation.

I presented the evidence in April 2025. On 17 November 2025, San Antonio became one of Hong Kong's first Partner Ports. The work continues.

26
[11:35–11:38 · 3 min] In April 2025, I presented an evidence-based proposal on the opportunity for a Hong Kong–San Antonio Green Shipping Corridor. On 17 November 2025, Hong Kong announced San Antonio as one of its first international Partner Ports. The Transport and Logistics Bureau later described the Cherry Express connection and said the two sides would continue exploring collaboration, including a Green Shipping Corridor. I want to be careful about the claim. Research did not single-handedly create the partnership, and a partner-port arrangement is not yet a completed green corridor. The contribution of research was to organise evidence, identify a plausible opportunity and help put a concrete proposal into the conversation. The honest conclusion

Science informs policy. It does not make policy alone.

Evidence can identify an opportunity and reduce uncertainty. Implementation still depends on institutions, economics, politics, values—and people working together.

27
[11:38–11:40 · 2 min] This case brings us back to the original question. Science helped measure why shipping decarbonisation matters. It helped compare possible pathways. It helped identify a route with a real commercial foundation and translate that evidence into a practical policy conversation. But science did not decide the investment, build the infrastructure, create demand or align institutions. Those steps depend on economics, regulation, politics, values and collaboration. That is the role I want you to remember: science does not stand outside policy and deliver a perfect answer. It makes the problem clearer, the choices more visible and the next step more credible.
